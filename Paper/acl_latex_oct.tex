\textbf{Our contributions are as follows:} \textbf{(1)} We organize MP-VRDU failures into three architecture-independent loci: representation, selection, and reasoning (\S\ref{sec:attribution_framework}). \textbf{(2)} We investigate these failure modes through controlled interventions and stratified comparisons in a unified pipeline, with experiments across document collections and independently developed model families (\S\ref{sec:methodology}--\S\ref{sec:findings}). \textbf{(3)} We connect the findings to qualitative examples and accuracy--cost comparisons to derive practical guidance for representation, retrieval, and reasoner choices under different task requirements and resource constraints (\S\ref{sec:design}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\paragraph{Design and Diagnostic Studies.} Studies of multimodal document question answering increasingly provide empirical guidance for system design. \citeauthor{ak2026empirical} compare input modalities, retrieval strategies, and agentic access on MMLongBench-Doc and LongDocURL, finding that the benefit of agentic processing depends on model capacity and benchmark. Their retrieval ablations also show that modality choice can matter more than retriever choice, and that lightweight reranking can match more elaborate retrieval pipelines. \citeauthor{feng2026docscope} evaluate page localization, region grounding, fact extraction, and answer verification separately; their oracle study identifies perception and extraction as major bottlenecks. These studies establish that design choices and intermediate failures warrant analysis beyond final answer accuracy.

\paragraph{Failure Mechanisms in RAG.} Related studies isolate mechanisms that inform individual pipeline choices. OHRBench shows that semantic and formatting errors introduced by OCR degrade downstream retrieval and generation~\citep{zhang2025ocr}. \citeauthor{cuconasu2024power} show that additional passages can help or harm depending on their relevance and position, while \citeauthor{jin2025long} find that increasing retrieval depth initially improves accuracy before hard negatives cause degradation. Context-faithful prompting improves adherence to supplied evidence and abstention behavior without additional training~\citep{zhou2023context}. These findings motivate examining conversion fidelity, evidence composition, and response calibration together in the multimodal, cross-page setting.

\paragraph{MP-VRDU Pipeline Designs.} MP-VRDU pipelines instantiate different approaches to acquiring and integrating evidence. M3DocRAG couples visual page retrieval with multimodal answer generation~\citep{cho2024m3docrag}, while MDocAgent combines specialized agents processing textual and visual evidence~\citep{han2025mdocagent}. SimpleDoc combines similarity retrieval with summary-based filtering and reranking, repeatedly loading pages into working memory until it can produce an answer~\citep{jain2025simpledoc}. DocTrace instead learns evidence localization and hierarchical evidence-graph reasoning through supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning~\citep{xiang2026doctrace}. These approaches show the value of evidence management, but leave open which design choices suffice for a given failure profile. Building on this literature, we derive design implications through controlled interventions on representation, selection, and reasoning, then test their joint utility in an attribution-guided reference pipeline, with deployment cost as a secondary consideration.

\section{Attribution Framework}
\label{sec:attribution_framework}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/framework.pdf}
\caption{The three failure loci and six mechanisms.}
\label{fig:framework}
\end{figure*}

We argue that MP-VRDU design can be decomposed into three fundamental loci defined irrespective of system architecture as \textbf{representation}, \textbf{selection}, and \textbf{reasoning}. All MP-VRDU techniques target failure at one or more of these loci. For example, Retrieval and adaptive reranking primarily address selection, while agentic techniques can span all three: gathering further evidence addresses selection, re-rendering a region addresses representation, and revising an answer addresses reasoning. This decomposition connects architectural choices to the failures they seek to minimize. Figure~\ref{fig:framework} summarises the loci and their associated mechanisms.

\subsection{Representation}
\label{sec:fm_representation}

Representation converts the document into the artifact consumed by later stages, such as embedded text, parser markdown, rendered pages, or visual crops. This locus concerns document conversion, while backbone operations after tokenization, including projection, resampling, and compression, belong to the reasoning loci.
\textbf{(1) Modality Ceiling.} A representation limits which signals are accessible at its available granularity: text may omit non-linguistic visual information, while images require sufficient resolution to expose fine detail.
\textbf{(2) Conversion Fidelity.} Conversion may degrade information that the modality could otherwise preserve, for example through OCR errors or corrupted table structure~\citep{zhang2025ocr}.

\subsection{Selection}
\label{sec:fm_selection}

Selection determines which represented document content is placed before the reasoner within the available context budget. It may operate over pages, sections, tables, or visual crops, in a single pass or through repeated acquisition.
\textbf{(1) Evidence Coverage.} Coverage measures the proportion of required evidence units included in the selected context; omission leaves the reasoner without information needed for the answer.
\textbf{(2) Distractor Exposure.} Exposure measures irrelevant or misleading content accompanying the required evidence, increasing the burden of identifying and using the relevant information even when coverage is complete.

\subsection{Reasoning}
\label{sec:fm_reasoning}

Reasoning maps the supplied evidence and instruction to a response, which may be a final answer, structured output, or an agentic tool call. When representation preserves the required evidence and selection supplies it, remaining failures concern how the reasoner interprets and acts on that evidence. Although such failures take many forms, we focus on two mechanisms particularly relevant to MP-VRDU:
\textbf{(1) Evidence Integration.} The reasoner may process individual evidence units correctly yet fail to combine them across pages or sources; long-context position effects can amplify this difficulty~\citep{liu2024lost}.
\textbf{(2) Response Calibration.} The decision to answer should reflect evidential support: failure includes unsupported answering (hallucination) and false abstention despite sufficient evidence. These opposing outcomes must be assessed jointly because greater willingness to answer can reduce false abstention while increasing unsupported responses~\citep{whitehead2022reliable}.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

We test the six failure mechanisms through controlled experiments, derive design implications from the results, and validate these implications at the system level. Appendix~\ref{app:full_experimental_detail} provides full experimental specifications.

\subsection{Attribution by Construction}
\label{sec:attribution}

We instantiate the attribution framework in a \textbf{single-pass retriever-generator} document answering pipeline. Its stages align with the three failure modes: \textit{encoding} determines the representation available to the system, \textit{retrieval} determines which pages reach the reasoner, and \textit{inference} determines how the supplied evidence is used to answer. The encoding stage converts each page to parser markdown, a rendered image, or both. The retrieval stage scores pages against the question and selects the top $k$ at page granularity; oracle conditions instead supply annotated gold pages. The inference stage gives a VLM only the selected pages, the question, and a task instruction, then produces an answer in one pass. Because the stages act once and in order, we can construct comparisons that change the representation, evidence set, or reasoning conditions relevant to a failure mechanism while holding the other stages fixed.

\subsection{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiment_design}

\paragraph{Representation.}
To test the \textit{modality ceiling}, we supply gold pages as embedded text extracted by PyMuPDF (\textbf{T}), PaddleOCR-VL markdown (\textbf{TL})~\citep{cui2025paddleocr}, markdown plus page images (\textbf{TLV}), or images alone (\textbf{V}), and compare results by evidence source. To test \textit{conversion fidelity}, we compare PaddleOCR-VL with MinerU 2.5~\citep{niu2026mineru2} and Unlimited-OCR~\citep{yin2026unlimited} within \textbf{TL} and \textbf{TLV}, then sweep the \texttt{low}, \texttt{med}, and \texttt{high} image presets (see Appendix~\ref{app:representation}) within \textbf{V} and \textbf{TLV}. The parser and resolution changes are evaluated separately. The default is \textbf{TLV} with PaddleOCR-VL and \texttt{med}: pages are rendered at 200 DPI and downsampled to at most 501,760 pixels per page (a median of 456 visual tokens).

\paragraph{Selection.}
To test \textit{evidence coverage}, we begin with the complete gold-page set for each multi-page question. ColQwen3, a ColPali-style visual retriever~\citep{faysse2025colpali}, ranks every page against the question. We remove either the highest- or lowest-ranked gold page to test whether retrieval rank predicts the cost of omission, then retain only one of those pages to examine more severe losses of coverage. To test \textit{distractor exposure}, we retain the complete gold set and add the top $k$ non-gold pages by retrieval rank, for $k=0,\ldots,18$. These pages provide plausible competing context while annotated coverage remains fixed. Each intervention is compared with the complete-gold condition on the same questions.

\paragraph{Reasoning.}
The default reasoner is Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct~\citep{team2025qwen3} in \texttt{bf16} with greedy decoding. To examine \textit{evidence integration}, we supply gold pages and compare accuracy by evidence-page and evidence-source count across Qwen3-VL model sizes from 2B to 32B. To test \textit{response calibration} with the 8B model, we compare \texttt{none}, \texttt{cot}, \texttt{abstain}, and \texttt{abstain\_cot} prompt modes on answerable questions with gold pages and unanswerable questions with BM25's top three pages. \texttt{cot} requests step-by-step reasoning followed by a final answer prefixed with ``Answer:'', while \texttt{abstain} requests the exact response ``Not answerable'' when the question cannot be answered from the evidence provided. The \texttt{abstain\_cot} condition combines both instructions. The output limit is 256 tokens, or 2048 for prompts containing CoT.

\paragraph{Generalization.}
We use Google's Gemma-3~\cite{team2025gemma} as an independently developed alternative to Alibaba's Qwen3-VL, with distinct training data and model-development pipelines, to examine whether our findings extend beyond a single model family. On MMLongBench-Doc, we repeat the distractor-exposure and response-calibration experiments with Gemma-3-12B, and the evidence-integration experiment across Gemma-3 model sizes.

\subsection{Dataset and Evaluation}
\label{sec:dataset_evaluation}

MMLongBench-Doc~\citep{ma2024mmlongbench} contains 135 multimodal documents and questions annotated with evidence pages, evidence sources, and answerability. It is the only benchmark we are aware of whose annotations support the full representation, selection, and reasoning experiments. LongDocURL~\citep{deng2025longdocurl} provides a complementary test of modality-ceiling findings on exclusively born-digital documents, where answer-bearing content is often directly accessible in the text layer, even for figure questions. Its LVLM-generated, human-verified questions may further favour evidence recoverable through this construction pipeline, while its annotated evidence pages need not form a minimal sufficient set. We therefore do not repeat the conversion-fidelity and evidence-coverage analyses on LongDocURL.

We use Gemini-2.5-Flash at temperature zero to judge answers. Given only the question, gold answer, unanswerable flag, and extracted model answer, the judge returns \texttt{correct}, \texttt{incorrect}, or \texttt{abstained}. It labels any refusal \texttt{abstained}, regardless of answerability; a refusal is credited as correct only for an unanswerable question. Accuracy is the proportion credited as correct, and paired transitions track correct-to-incorrect and incorrect-to-correct rates in our experiments. Judge agreement is 96.0\% with GPT-4o and 98.3\% with a human annotator on validation subsets.

\paragraph{Modality Ceiling: Evidence Accessibility Depends on Modality.}
A modality can only support questions whose required information it preserves. On MMLongBench-Doc, this effect is most apparent for chart and figure questions: \textbf{T} and \textbf{TL} show substantially higher refusal, whereas adding page images reduces abstention to below 10\% across evidence sources. The converse also holds: \textbf{V} remains less accurate than \textbf{TLV} when dense text or precise numerical values are required. Together, these results show that text and vision preserve complementary information. LongDocURL provides a contrasting case: its born-digital documents expose more usable text, so refusal remains below 10\% across representations and adding vision to \textbf{TL} improves overall accuracy by only 2.8 points, although figure questions still gain 7.1 points. The modality ceiling therefore depends on which task-relevant information remains unavailable in the chosen representation.

\paragraph{Conversion Fidelity: Complementary Modalities Reduce Conversion Sensitivity.}
Conversion errors become less consequential when the affected information remains accessible through another modality. This compensation works in both directions: visual input can recover information lost during parsing, while parser text can reduce dependence on visual resolution for fine-grained textual content. The results support this pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:fidelity}): adding page images reduces the accuracy spread across parsers from 15.7 to 4.2 points on digital documents, while increasing image resolution brings substantially larger gains under \textbf{V} than under \textbf{TLV} for text and table questions. The compensation is incomplete when the modalities preserve non-substitutable information, as seen in the continued sensitivity of scanned documents to parser quality and of figure questions to visual resolution. Conversion fidelity therefore matters most when degraded information cannot be recovered from a complementary modality.

\paragraph{Evidence Coverage: Omission Causes Large Accuracy Losses.}
Answer accuracy is highly sensitive to missing required evidence. Removing only one gold page reduces accuracy from 38.6\% to 18.4\% when the lowest-ranked page is removed and to 15.9\% when the highest-ranked page is removed (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}). Paired verdicts show that correct answers are broken far more often than incorrect answers are repaired, indicating that the remaining evidence rarely compensates for the omission. The similar losses under both removals further show that retrieval rank does not reliably predict the cost of excluding a required page. Coverage failure therefore depends primarily on whether necessary evidence is present, rather than where that evidence appears in the ranking.

\paragraph{Distractor Exposure: Multi-Hop Accuracy Drops Sharply with Added Context.}
Added non-gold evidence generally reduces accuracy, with multi-hop questions showing the sharpest early degradation (Figure~\ref{fig:distractor_k}). This pattern holds across backbones. For Qwen3-VL-8B, multi-hop accuracy drops from 43.9\% to 35.1\% after only three additional pages, while single-hop accuracy declines more gradually from 66.8\% to 57.2\% across the sweep. Gemma3-12B shows the same overall trend, with multi-hop accuracy falling from 35.4\% to 23.6\%, although its single-hop accuracy is more sensitive to added context, decreasing from 60.3\% to 40.7\%. Because the complete gold set remains available throughout, these losses reflect difficulty using required evidence amid competing context rather than missing evidence. Extra context can both help and hurt: it may fix some incorrect answers by adding useful information, but can also break correct answers by introducing competing information. Distractor exposure therefore makes it harder to use the right evidence consistently, with different sensitivity across backbones.

Just three additional pages lower accuracy from 66.8\% to 63.1\% on single-hop questions and from 43.9\% to 35.1\% on multi-hop questions (Figure~\ref{fig:distractor_k}). Because evidence coverage remains fixed, the larger multi-hop loss points to difficulty using the required evidence amid added context. Similar degradation from high-ranked non-answer passages and accumulated hard negatives has been reported previously~\citep{cuconasu2024power,jin2025long}. Paired verdicts show that added pages both break correct answers and repair incorrect ones. Additional document context may define a term, resolve a reference, or connect facts that were difficult to interpret in isolation. It can also introduce a plausible but misleading partial match that draws the reasoner away from the required evidence. This competition is especially consequential for multi-hop questions, where the reasoner must decide which facts to combine across pages.

\paragraph{Evidence Integration: Scaling Helps, but the Multi-Hop Gap Persists.}
Larger reasoners improve both single-hop and multi-hop accuracy, but do not eliminate the gap between them (Figure~\ref{fig:integration_scale}). For Qwen3-VL, multi-hop accuracy rises from 29.0\% at 2B to 49.9\% at 32B, while single-hop accuracy increases from 51.0\% to 74.4\%. The gap remains roughly unchanged, showing that scaling improves overall evidence use but does not specifically resolve the difficulty of combining evidence across pages or sources. Gemma3 shows the same overall separation between single-hop and multi-hop performance, although the gains vary across evidence types. One likely reason is that scaling improves the processing of individual evidence units as well as multi-hop reasoning, while multi-hop questions still require additional operations to locate, align, and combine information across sources. Thus, greater model capacity raises overall performance, but does not remove the extra burden introduced by evidence integration.
% Multi-hop accuracy increases with model scale when the complete gold evidence is supplied. Scaling Qwen3-VL from 2B to 32B raises accuracy from 29.0\% to 49.9\% (Figure~\ref{fig:integration_scale}). With representation and selection fixed, this gain reflects improved use of supplied evidence. Improvements occur across all evidence sources, although their trajectories differ. Multi-hop chart accuracy shows the largest gain, from 14.7\% to 40.2\%, but remains the lowest at 32B. For layout questions, single-hop accuracy plateaus at 4B while multi-hop accuracy continues to improve. Overall single-hop accuracy also rises from 51.0\% to 74.4\%, indicating that scaling benefits evidence use generally rather than integration alone. Gemma3 replicates the overall gains in both groups, although its multi-hop improvements are less consistent across evidence sources. Greater reasoner capacity therefore improves performance on questions requiring evidence combination, although these comparisons do not separate gains in locating individual facts from gains in combining them.

\paragraph{Response Calibration: Reasoning Before Abstaining Limits False Refusal.}
Abstention prompting substantially increases refusal on unanswerable questions, but also makes the reasoner more likely to refuse answerable ones (Figure~\ref{fig:reasoning_abstain_cot}). This trade-off appears across both backbones. For Qwen3-VL-8B, \texttt{abstain} raises unanswerable refusal from 45.5\% to 74.6\%, but also increases false refusal from 6.2\% to 27.7\% and reduces answerable accuracy from 49.5\% to 40.0\%. Gemma3-12B shows the same shift, with unanswerable refusal increasing from 43.9\% to 73.4\% while false refusal rises from 6.3\% to 35.1\%. Adding CoT changes this balance differently across models. For Qwen3-VL-8B, \texttt{abstain\_cot} reduces false refusal to 19.9\%, raises answerable accuracy to 50.7\%, and further improves unanswerable refusal to 85.5\%. For Gemma3-12B, false refusal falls to 32.5\%, but answerable accuracy improves by only 0.5 points and unanswerable refusal declines to 70.1\%. These results show that reasoning before abstaining can recover supported answers, but its benefits vary across backbones, and false refusal remains substantially above baseline for both models.

% CoT alone changes accuracy and refusal rates little relative to \texttt{none} (Figure~\ref{fig:reasoning_abstain_cot}). The \texttt{abstain} instruction instead raises unanswerable refusal from 13.2\% to 76.1\%, but also increases false refusal to 25.8\% and lowers answerable accuracy from 55.2\% to 43.8\%. Evidence can appear insufficient when the answer is difficult to locate or assemble, so a general instruction to abstain can also suppress supported answers. Combining the instructions in \texttt{abstain\_cot} raises unanswerable refusal further to 85.5\%, reduces false refusal to 19.9\%, and recovers answerable accuracy to 53.0\%. Although Qwen3-VL already incorporates CoT data during pretraining~\citep{team2025qwen3}, explicitly requesting step-by-step reasoning before answering still changes how it responds to the abstention instruction. The effects of prompting however may also depend on the model's training and instruction tuning, so its transfer to other backbones requires validation.

\section{Practical Discussion}
\label{sec:design}

We discuss the findings of \S\ref{sec:findings} across the three failure modes and their implications for designing and deploying document question-answering systems, drawing on qualitative examples (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism_examples}) and accuracy--cost comparisons (Table~\ref{tab:deploy}).

\subsection{Representation}
\label{sec:implications_representation}
Our representation analysis shows that failures arise when the input does not preserve task-relevant information, suggesting that complementary modalities are needed to recover missing evidence. Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism_examples} illustrates two such failures: text-only input loses visual attributes such as icon colour, while parser errors distort textual evidence that remains recoverable from page images. However, adding more modalities does not necessarily improve performance. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:deploy}, embedded text with images (TV) achieves accuracy comparable to parser text with images (TLV), 50.1\% versus 49.5\%, while requiring fewer tokens (959 versus 1,333). Visual processing also incurs substantial prefill latency (26.3s for TLV versus 1.8s for TL), highlighting the cost of richer representations. Deployment Suggestions. Systems should prioritise reliable embedded text, add images when visual information is needed or extraction is unreliable, and re-parse when existing text is inadequate. For local deployment, modality choice and evidence selection depth should be jointly configured to balance accuracy, memory requirements, and inference latency.
% Our representation findings support matching modalities to the document types and task at hand. The icon-colour and company-name examples illustrate how vision can expose otherwise inaccessible attributes and compensate for parser errors (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism_examples}). These benefits carry processing costs: visual input incurs substantial prefill latency despite its relatively small token count, while embedded text paired with images achieves accuracy comparable to parser text plus images without requiring parsing (Table~\ref{tab:deploy}). The appropriate representation therefore depends on which information the task requires and how reliably each channel preserves it. Some tasks require exact values and field associations, as in key information extraction, while others may rely on broader textual or visual cues, as in document classification. These requirements can be established through domain knowledge or controlled comparisons on representative documents and tasks. Parser output remains susceptible to omissions and distortions, so complementary modalities can provide useful redundancy when extraction loses required information. Where embedded text is adequate, however, additional parsing may offer little benefit. Representation and selection depth should also be configured jointly, since richer inputs increase the cost of supplying more evidence. The objective is to preserve task-relevant information reliably within the available budget.

\subsection{Selection}
\label{sec:implications_selection}
Our selection analysis shows that failures arise when necessary evidence is omitted or irrelevant context interferes with otherwise sufficient evidence, suggesting that selection should prioritise collective evidence coverage while controlling distractor exposure. Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism_examples} illustrates both failures: missing complementary pages prevents correct evidence integration, while additional context can either disrupt or restore an answer. These findings are consistent with prior observations on distracting passages and hard negatives in RAG~\citep{cuconasu2024power,jin2025long}, while highlighting the value of document context for interpreting evidence relationships. Structured navigation, summary-based reranking, and iterative retrieval offer potential ways to balance these competing requirements \citep{sun2025docagent,jain2025simpledoc}. Table~\ref{tab:deploy} further shows that ColQwen3 improves R@5 over BM25 (81.0\% versus 58.1\%), but requires GPU memory and greater query latency (110 versus 0.3 ms). \textbf{Deployment Suggestions.} For frequently queried document collections, stronger visual retrievers may justify their indexing and memory costs, whereas lightweight text retrievers remain attractive for resource-constrained or infrequently queried collections. Since retrieval and subsequent reasoning costs recur for each query, retrieval depth and refinement strategies should be evaluated by end-to-end answer quality and latency rather than recall alone.
% Our selection findings suggest that evidence should be selected according to how it supports the answer collectively, rather than how relevant each item appears in isolation. The qualitative examples show that complementary evidence can be jointly necessary and additional context can either disrupt or restore correct answers (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism_examples}). The observed degradation is consistent with studies of highly ranked non-answer passages and hard negatives in RAG~\citep{cuconasu2024power,jin2025long}. In document understanding, however, context beyond the answer-bearing evidence may clarify relationships needed to interpret it. Selection should therefore preserve useful document context while controlling competing information, whether operating over passages, visual regions, or sections. Structured navigation, summary-based reranking, and iterative query refinement offer ways to pursue this balance beyond a fixed similarity ranking~\citep{sun2025docagent,jain2025simpledoc}. The tested visual retrievers achieve higher recall than text-based alternatives at additional indexing, query, and GPU cost (Table~\ref{tab:deploy}). Indexing can be amortised across repeated queries, whereas retrieval, reranking, and reasoning costs recur. Stronger retrieval may reduce the depth needed for coverage, while iterative acquisition adds model calls. Retriever choice, context refinement, and stopping criteria should therefore be evaluated jointly against answer quality and total processing cost.

\subsection{Reasoning}
\label{sec:implications_reasoning}

Our reasoning findings suggest matching model capacity and inference strategy to the operations required by the task. The qualitative examples illustrate failures within an evidence-combination chain and a supported answer suppressed by an abstention instruction (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism_examples}). Beyond a single reasoning pass, potential approaches include Reflexion~\citep{shinn2023reflexion}, which retains reflections on feedback to guide subsequent attempts, and Self-Consistency~\citep{wang2023self}, which aggregates answers across sampled reasoning paths. Separating answer construction from evidence-based verification offers another way to examine whether an apparent lack of support reflects missing information or reasoning difficulty. These strategies require additional computation, and their effectiveness depends on feedback quality and the model's ability to recognise errors. They should therefore be assessed on the intended task alongside answer accuracy and refusal behaviour. The configurations in Table~\ref{tab:deploy} further show that model family and precision affect accuracy--cost trade-offs: quantisation largely preserves accuracy with a smaller weight footprint, but does not necessarily reduce latency. A memory-constrained system may favour a quantised, more capable model, while a latency-constrained system may favour fewer reasoning steps or a faster backbone. Capacity, precision, prompting, and verification should consequently be configured together, reserving additional computation for operations where it improves supported answering.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Our study shows how representation, selection, and reasoning shape MP-VRDU performance: complementary modalities preserve access to evidence, selection must balance coverage with useful document context, and reasoner capacity and prompting affect evidence use and refusal behaviour. These findings provide guidance for matching system components to task requirements and resource constraints. Future work can build on this guidance to target the failures that limit a given workload, choosing interventions according to whether information is lost during conversion, omitted during selection, or misused during reasoning. In adaptive systems, these distinctions can inform when to revisit the document, acquire complementary evidence, or invest in further reasoning and verification. They also motivate evaluations that connect component choices to supported answering, refusal behaviour, and computational cost, helping future methods demonstrate both where their gains originate and when those gains are practically valuable.
